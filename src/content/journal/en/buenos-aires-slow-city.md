Buenos Aires does not reveal itself to the hurried. It is a city of thresholds — the half-open door of a *conventillo* in San Telmo, the courtyard glimpsed behind the wrought iron of a Recoleta *petit hôtel*, the bookshop that was once a theatre. To know it, you have to walk, and you have to keep its hours.

## Keep the porteño clock

Lunch begins late and lingers. Dinner rarely starts before nine. The mistake most visitors make is to fight this rhythm; the pleasure lies in surrendering to it. Mornings are for museums and long walks, afternoons for a café and a book, evenings for the table.

> The city rewards those who look up: domes, balconies, mansards — a Paris imagined from across the Atlantic.

## Three neighbourhoods, three tempos

**Recoleta** is the grand salon: French-inspired façades, the Museo Nacional de Bellas Artes, and tree-lined avenues made for the slowest of strolls.

**San Telmo** is the archive: cobblestones, antique dealers, and a Sunday fair that is best visited early, before the crowds.

**Palermo** is the conversation: design studios, independent galleries, and some of the most interesting kitchens in the city.

## How I would spend a first day

- Morning at the Museo Nacional de Bellas Artes, followed by a walk through Recoleta.
- A late lunch, unhurried, in a classic *bodegón*.
- An afternoon among the bookshops of Avenida Corrientes.
- Dinner in Palermo, after nine, naturally.
